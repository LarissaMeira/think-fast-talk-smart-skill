# Think Fast Talk Smart — Strategic Communication Skill

Based on the principles of Matt Abrahams, professor of Strategic Communication
at Stanford Graduate School of Business.

---

## Core Principle

> "Communication is critical to success in your professional and personal lives."

Effective communication is not about talking more. It is about structuring
well, listening actively, and adapting your message to whoever is in the room.

---

## 01. Communication Structures

### PREP — To defend a position or present an idea
Use when you need to argue, propose something, or respond to an objection.

```
P — Point    → Lead with your main point first
R — Reason   → Give the reason that supports that point
E — Example  → Give a concrete, specific example
P — Point    → Return to the main point to close
```

**When to use:** pitches, commercial proposals, meetings with decision-makers,
defending recommendations.

**Example applied (AI consulting context):**
- Point: "The absence of a structured database is the core problem."
- Reason: "Without it, every hire starts from scratch with no history or criteria."
- Example: "In the last expansion to Rio, it took 6 weeks to find qualified
  professionals because there was no record of previous candidates."
- Point: "That is why building that database is the foundation of everything."

---

### What? So What? Now What? — For updates, feedback, and introductions
Use when you need to inform, provide context, or transition between ideas.

```
What?      → What is happening / what is the fact
So What?   → Why it matters to the person listening
Now What?  → What is the next step or expected action
```

**When to use:** meeting summaries, follow-up emails, project updates, team
feedback, opening a presentation.

## 02. Communicating with Leadership and Executives

### Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF)
Executives do not read slides — they read titles. They do not wait until the
end to understand the point. Always lead with the conclusion.

**Practical rule:** if you removed everything from your message except the
first sentence, the decision-maker should still understand what you are asking
or proposing.

**Before:** "So, looking at the operational data, we noticed the screening
process has some manual steps that take up team time, and that led to the
idea of automating..."

**After:** "We can reduce screening time by 70% with automation. Here is how."

---

### Lingo vs. Jargon
- **Lingo:** technical language used with people who share that context.
  Demonstrates competence. Use with technical peers.
- **Jargon:** technical language used with people who do not share that context.
  Creates distance and confusion. Avoid with non-technical leadership.

> "Jargon monoxide — it suffocates communication." — Huggy Rao, Stanford

**Practical translation guide for AI consulting with leadership:**

| Avoid                        | Use with leadership                              |
|------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| "We'll implement a RAG"      | "We'll build a knowledge base the system         |
|                              | consults automatically"                          |
| "LLM integrated into ERP"    | "The system will read your ERP data and          |
|                              | answer questions about it"                       |
| "Agent deployment"           | "We'll put a digital assistant to work           |
|                              | inside that process"                             |
| "Fine-tuned model"           | "A system trained specifically on your data"     |
| "Vector database"            | "A smart search layer over your documents"       |

---

### Detail vs. Concision
This is a real tension. The rule is: **follow the emotion you want to create.**

- If you want **deep engagement and an emotional decision** → more detail,
  more story, more concrete example.
- If you are **relaying data or technical steps** → concise, direct, no stories.

## 03. Managing Anxiety Before Important Meetings

### The ABC Method
Apply before meetings with decision-makers, presentations, or difficult conversations:

**A — Affect (Emotion)**
Acknowledge that anxiety is normal. About 85% of people feel it. It is a signal
that the meeting matters. Reframe: anxiety equals excitement about something
important.

**B — Behavior (Physical)**
Diaphragmatic breathing: inhale for 4 counts, exhale for 8 counts.
The impact lives in the long exhale. Do this in the 10 seconds before you begin.

**C — Cognition (Thought)**
Replace negative self-talk with a simple, true affirmation. It does not need
to be grand. Example: *"I have something of value to bring to this conversation."*

## 04. Active Listening and Paraphrasing

Paraphrasing is not just polite. It is a **fidelity check** — it confirms you
understood what was said before acting on it.

**How to practice:**
- At the end of each client statement, ask yourself: "What is the bottom line
  of what this person just said?"
- Restate it out loud: "If I understood correctly, what you are saying is..."
- This validates the speaker AND corrects misunderstandings before they become problems.

**Why this matters in a commercial context:**
A well-run discovery depends on paraphrasing before proposing. Whoever proposes
first sounds like a vendor. Whoever paraphrases first sounds like a strategic partner.

## 05. Gestures and Presence

- Start with descriptive gestures: mirror what you are saying
  ("big opportunity" → expansive gesture).
- Graduate to emphatic gestures: add emphasis without a direct word correlate.
- Never script gestures. Practice speaking and moving at the same time.

**Practice technique:**
Record an audio of yourself walking through the meeting. Listen with earbuds and
walk around practicing only the gestures — you do not need to think about the
words because you are already hearing them.

## 06. How to Apply This Skill

### Before the meeting — preparing what to say
When the user needs to prepare their message:
1. Identify the primary goal of the meeting: what do you want the decision-maker
   to do or decide by the end?
2. Apply BLUF: what is the one sentence that summarizes everything?
3. Build the body using PREP or What / So What / Now What.
4. Identify 1 concrete example with a real number or real story.
5. Anticipate objections and prepare responses using PREP.
6. Suggest an anxiety affirmation if relevant.

### After the meeting — communication analysis
When the user describes how the meeting went or shares notes or a transcript:
1. Assess whether the main point was stated first or buried at the end.
2. Check for technical jargon where business language was needed.
3. Identify whether paraphrasing happened before proposing.
4. Point out where PREP or What / So What / Now What would have made the message
   sharper. Show the rewritten version.
5. Suggest 1 specific, concrete adjustment for the next meeting.

---

## 07. Tone and Posture of This Skill

This skill should always sound like a **strategic coach**, not a grammar corrector.
The goal is not linguistic perfection. It is helping the user **sound like a
strategic partner**, not a technical expert trying to sell a solution.

Every analysis must end with 1 concrete, actionable step to apply in the next conversation.
